FlyBrain Reservoir

In 2024, FlyWire and an international consortium published the first complete wiring diagram of a fly brain. Janelia FlyEM, the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, the University of Cambridge and Google Research have since gone further, completing the male CNS connectome — brain and ventral nerve cord, seamlessly wired together, every neuron reconstructed and every synapse annotated. Below, that same circuit takes on real jobs. Its flagship task is Forecast a trend: paste in an actual sequence of numbers — sales, prices, readings, anything ordered — and its optic lobes read it once, step by step, while a trained readout learns to predict each next value and then keeps going past the end of what you gave it. Switch to Ride a bicycle to watch signals descend from its central complex into a modeled nerve cord, where a population of motor neurons learns, attempt after attempt, to keep a bicycle upright by trial, error and reward alone. Or switch to Interpolate a function to see it read a signal back from a few labeled samples in one shot. In every case, only a small trainable readout changes — the rest of the recurrent circuit never does.

This is not the downloaded connectome — that requires authenticated access to the full synapse-level reconstruction (tens of millions of synapses in the nerve cord alone). What runs below is a statistically faithful stand-in: regional neuron proportions, feedforward sensory pathways, recurrent hubs (central complex, mushroom body) and now a distinct nerve-cord module fed by descending neurons are drawn from the published connectomes' own architecture, then generated at a scale a browser can simulate live.

Task: ride a bicycle

A simplified lean-and-steer bicycle: it falls over unless something actively corrects it. The brain feels its lean angle and lean rate in its central complex, sends that command descending into the modeled nerve cord, and a small population of nerve-cord motor neurons outputs a single steering torque — the only synapses that learn.

Each generation, the brain's descending-neuron weights are perturbed several ways (evolution strategy / reward-modulated search), each perturbation gets one attempt, and the weights move toward whichever variations survived longest and stayed most upright. The dashed line marks a full, uncrashed run.

The bicycle itself is a simplified single-degree-of-freedom lean model (gravity torque vs. a speed-scaled steering correction, plus damping and disturbance) — not the full multi-body dynamics of a real bicycle. It keeps the physics honest enough to be genuinely hard without a working controller, while staying fast enough to simulate hundreds of attempts per second in a browser.

Task: forecast a trend

Paste any ordered sequence of real numbers. The brain reads it once, values feeding into its optic lobes step by step alongside a sense of position in time, and a trained readout learns to predict each next value from what came before. Validate it against data you hold back, or point it past the end of what you gave it.

No dates needed — just the values, in order, evenly spaced (daily, monthly, whatever your series actually is).

This is a general-purpose learning demonstration, not financial, medical or safety-critical forecasting software. It finds patterns in whatever you give it, including patterns that won't repeat — nothing here should drive a high-stakes decision on its own. The reservoir sees each value plus its position in time, predicts one step ahead, and — beyond your last known point — feeds its own predictions back in to keep going, so errors can compound the further out it forecasts.

Explore: the edge of chaos

This scan fixes the leak rate high (fast, non-leaky updates) so the transition shows clearly. Too low a spectral radius and the network's memory fades before it can hold useful context; push it too high and activity turns chaotic and washes out the signal. The sweet spot in between — the "edge of chaos" — is where biological circuits are hypothesized to operate too. Your leak-rate slider above shows the other side of this: a slow, heavily-damped brain stays stable even at high ρ, which is part of why the low-leak default fits so well.
